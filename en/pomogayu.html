What a cardiologist can help with

Outpatient and diagnostic cardiology at Caraps Medline, Tbilisi. Consultations in Georgian, Russian and English.

Severe chest pain lasting more than a few minutes, sudden breathlessness or fainting — call 112, don’t wait for an appointment.

What people usually come in with

High or fluctuating blood pressure

High blood pressure often goes unnoticed for years while it strains the heart, blood vessels, kidneys and brain. A cardiologist will check whether your pressure is really high (sometimes that takes 24-hour monitoring), look for causes and choose treatment. If you already take tablets and the numbers stay high, that’s a reason to come in too.

Skipped beats, a racing or unusually slow heartbeat

A “skipped” beat, thumps or a racing pulse can be harmless — or a sign of an arrhythmia. To find out, you need an ECG and sometimes a recording of your rhythm over 24 hours or longer (a Holter monitor), which catches irregular beats that don’t happen every minute.

Chest pain, pressure or discomfort

Chest pain isn’t always the heart — the spine, muscles, stomach or anxiety can cause it too. A cardiologist’s job is to rule out heart causes and order tests if needed. If the pain is severe, lasts more than a few minutes, spreads to the arm, jaw or back, or comes with breathlessness or a cold sweat — call 112.

Breathlessness on everyday exertion, swollen ankles

Getting breathless on exertion that used to be easy, swollen ankles by evening, needing extra pillows to sleep — these are reasons to check your heart, as they can be signs of heart failure or valve disease. An echocardiogram — a heart ultrasound — often gives the answer.

High cholesterol or blood sugar, heart attacks or strokes in the family

High cholesterol, diabetes, smoking, excess weight, and heart attacks or strokes in close relatives at a young age all raise the risk of heart and vascular disease. A cardiologist will assess your risk and explain what is worth changing — lifestyle, treatment or both.

Dizziness and fainting

Fainting, or feeling you’re about to, can be linked to heart rhythm or blood pressure. It’s worth discussing with a cardiologist — especially if it happens during exertion.

Several medicines at once — how they work together

When you take several medicines, it matters how they combine with each other and with other drugs and supplements. This can be reviewed at a visit — bring a list of everything you take, with doses.

A heart check before surgery or sport

Before planned surgery, a surgeon or anaesthetist may refer you to a cardiologist — especially older people and those with heart conditions. A heart check is also worth it before starting intense training if you have symptoms or risk factors.

Tests a cardiologist may order

ECG
a recording of the heart’s electrical activity; takes a few minutes.
Holter monitor
a recording of your heart rhythm over 24 hours or longer, during everyday life.
24-hour blood pressure monitoring
a device measures your blood pressure automatically at regular intervals through the day and night.
Echocardiogram
a heart ultrasound: chamber size, valve function, how the heart contracts.
Exercise test
how the heart copes with exertion — on a treadmill or exercise bike.
Blood tests
cholesterol and its fractions, blood sugar and other markers — as needed.

Which of these you need is decided at your visit.
